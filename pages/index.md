    Hi. My name is Hannah Vogel. I am an archaeologist and my PhD research is on disability in history and archaeology.

    I am the curator of this exhibition titled Disability Heritage: Archaeology with Difference

    This exhibition is both an online and physical museum display. The display is at the Gale History Museum at Macquarie University, Sydney Australia from mid November 2026 until March 2027. The online exhibition will be around for a lot longer than that.

    In this video I will tell you more about the exhibition I have curated, and some of the accessibility features we have incorporated to the display and website.

    The introductory panel reads;

    Disability Heritage: Archaeology with Difference

    Disability is part of our history. However, archaeology is often ableist, overlooking, or worse, devaluing disabled experiences.

    This display invites you to think differently.

    If we place disability at the centre of our story, the object on the top shelf can represent the use of mobility aids, despite not usually being understood as disability representation. The middle shelf challenges the ideal of a bodily norm. It features a diversity of bodies, both human and divine, demonstrating difference across cultures and throughout time. The feeding vessels on the bottom shelf are evidence of community and care. These objects, created thousands of years apart, are products of differences in bodily experience within a community.

    This display is not only about disability, it is for the disability community. The accessibility ‘features’ are disability heritage just as much as the archaeological material behind the glass.

    In the museum display, the wall panel includes:

    - a tactile fabrication of scene on the first object,
    - 3D prints of objects on the second and third shelves
    - A QR code and an ipad to watch this video or access this website.
    - We also have nearby a Braille and Large-print guide of the panel text and labels.

    For our website, alongside this video, we also have interactive 3D models, customisable text size, audio-visual descriptions and high-resolution photographs with alternative text descriptions, which you can zoom in or explore the artefacts from multiple perspectives.

    In the display itself, there are three shelves.

    The top shelf has one object, a pelike. This pelike depicts the use of a mobility aid: a staff or cane. In ancient Greece, staff-like objects were also symbols of power, authority and justice. Archaeological narratives are selective about how these artefacts are interpreted, often rejecting the possibility of disability coinciding with symbols of power.

    On the middle shelve there are seven small figurines or amulets.

    Figurines were made for various reasons and were often accessible to many people. These differently shaped bodies are votive offerings or amulets used for protection, ensuring health and/or fertility, invoking the divine, or for afterlife beliefs. These are an important reflection of the reality of diversity, rather than an ‘ideal’ body.

    The bottom shelf has three objects. These feeding vessels, despite differences in design over time, were made to support the needs of people. They represent difference creating change in [material] culture.

    This project has included many members of the disability community throughout all stages of its development. Acknowledgments are featured on the website.

    This exhibition is funded by the National Disability Research Partnership’s inaugural Disability Research Leadership Program, and is part of my PhD project supported by the Commonwealth through an Australian Government Research Training Program.

    If you want to learn more about my research or other projects, you can find my profiles on the website about page.
